THE CENTRAL QUESTION

What does turbulence do
to a cloud of droplets?

Small droplets do not respond instantly to changes in surrounding airflow. Their inertia, settling and interactions with vortical structures can create droplet-rich clusters, depleted regions and relative motion between neighbouring droplets.

These mechanisms matter to the warm-cloud condensation–coalescence bottleneck: the intermediate size range where condensational growth slows before gravitational collection becomes efficient. My experiments investigate the mechanical side of this problem, without claiming to reproduce the full thermodynamic complexity of clouds.

RESEARCH SHOWCASE

From a single vortex to
turbulent droplet fields

Real experimental recordings, flow-field measurements and analysis from my doctoral research. Each study moves from a directly observable flow to a more complete description of droplet behaviour.

01 / CHARACTERISATION

Vortex-ring generation & characterisation

Canonical air vortex rings provide a repeatable way to isolate the effect of one coherent vortical structure. High-speed imaging and time-resolved particle image velocimetry (TR-PIV) resolve the flow as the ring propagates.

Circulation, core radius, peak vorticity, ring diameter and convection velocity define the relevant dynamical scales.

02 / INTERACTION

A moving vortex meets polydisperse droplets

The passing vortex induces different responses across a droplet population. Finite inertia, gravity, initial position and interaction history determine whether a droplet undergoes marked deflection, near-core motion or a weak far-field encounter.

Direct recordings connect the dynamics of a controlled flow structure to the redistribution of droplets in its surroundings.

03 / LAGRANGIAN DYNAMICS

Following droplets in the vortex frame of reference

Droplet centroids are detected and linked across frames using particle tracking velocimetry (PTV). Their motion is examined both in the laboratory frame and relative to the translating, non-rotating vortex centre.

The vortex-frame transformation separates overall vortex translation from the droplet’s relative displacement, velocity and acceleration. Near-core encounters generally produce more pronounced curvature and radial migration.

04 / SPATIAL ORGANISATION

Preferential concentration, clusters & voids

Voronoï tessellation quantifies local droplet organisation without arbitrary spatial bins. Normalised cell areas are compared against a matched random Poisson process to distinguish excess small-area clusters from large-area voids.

The distributions also reveal where clustering is most likely relative to the moving vortex, including radial and angular variations.

05 / PAIR DYNAMICS

From clustering to collision-relevant encounters

The radial distribution function (RDF) measures excess droplet-pair proximity; radial relative velocity (RRV) measures their mutual motion. Combining these with a geometric contact factor produces a kinematic near-contact encounter kernel.

The representative 80 cm Hg interaction yielded a probability-weighted kernel approximately 273 times its adopted gravity-only differential-settling reference under the study's measurement and modelling definitions.

Important distinction: Planar imaging did not directly measure droplet contact or subsequent coalescence. The kernel is a geometric, collision-relevant estimate—not an observed physical collision or coalescence rate.

06 / TURBULENT SHEAR FLOW

Polydisperse droplets in a turbulent boundary layer

A barrier–spire–roughness arrangement generated an approximately metre-thick, rough-wall turbulent boundary layer (TBL). The carrier flow was characterised at free-stream velocities of 1–4 m/s; droplet measurements were carried out at 3 m/s in the outer layer.

The analysis finds persistent non-random droplet organisation after prolonged exposure to mean shear and turbulent fluctuations. These experiments are an atmospheric-boundary-layer-like laboratory analogue, not a complete reproduction of atmospheric clouds.
