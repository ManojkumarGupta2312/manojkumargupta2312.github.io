Manojkumar I. Gupta

From a single vortex to the complex physics of cloud droplets.

The scientific challenge. In warm clouds, condensation becomes increasingly slow before collision–coalescence is efficient. Turbulence may help bridge this droplet-growth bottleneck by gathering droplets into clusters and changing how rapidly neighbours approach. But in real clouds, interacting vortices, a broad range of droplet sizes and different motion histories make these effects difficult to separate.

My experimental approach. I first generate and characterise an isolated air vortex ring using high-speed imaging and time-resolved particle image velocimetry. I then track polydisperse water droplets in the vortex frame and use Voronoï analysis to reveal clusters and voids. Pair-distribution and relative-velocity statistics provide estimates of collision-relevant near-contact encounters, rather than directly measured coalescence. Finally, I examine droplets in a rough-wall turbulent boundary layer, where sustained shear and interacting structures make the problem more complex. This approach connects measurable vortex-scale mechanisms with turbulence-scale observations without treating laboratory flows as real clouds.

RESEARCH IN MOTION

Inside the experiments.

Original high-speed recordings and analysis from my PhD research. Short explanations, real measurements—and more questions worth exploring.

01 / EXPERIMENT

Build a vortex. Measure its flow.

Controlled vortical structures make it possible to investigate droplet response one mechanism at a time.

01 EXPERIMENT DESIGN

A laboratory built to ask one question.

Together with Prof. Narsing Kumar Jha and the laboratory team, I helped develop the vortex-ring–droplet experiment at IIT Delhi—from material selection and fabrication to alignment, calibration and high-speed measurements.

02 FLOW VISUALISATION

First, make the invisible visible.

Fog-seeded images bring a travelling vortex ring into view and expose the evolving structure of the flow. Its motion is simple to generate, but the particle response is not.

03 FLOW CHARACTERISATION

Looking beyond the vortex shape.

Time-resolved particle image velocimetry connects the visible structure with velocity, vorticity, circulation and vortex-core scales—the quantities that govern the ensuing droplet interaction.

04 DROPLET–VORTEX INTERACTION

A single vortex. Many different journeys.

Watch how polydisperse droplets respond as a ring passes through. Near-core deflection, fly-by motion and comparatively weak far-field encounters emerge from the same coherent flow.

02 / INTERACTION

Follow the droplets.

From individual paths to preferential concentration and near-contact pair motion.

05 LAGRANGIAN DYNAMICS

Following a droplet changes the view.

In the translating vortex frame of reference, tracking reveals how each droplet moves relative to the coherent structure—not just across the laboratory image.

06 PREFERENTIAL CONCENTRATION

Patterns hiding in a cloud of points.

Voronoï tessellation exposes droplet-rich clusters and depleted voids. Their locations change across and around the moving vortex, revealing more than an average concentration can.

07 PAIR DYNAMICS

Closer together—but moving towards each other?

Clustering is not enough to establish encounters. Pair concentration and radial relative velocity are combined to estimate kinematic near-contact kernels, while keeping physical collision and coalescence distinct.

03 / TURBULENCE

Bring the question into shear turbulence.

A rough-wall boundary layer adds sustained turbulent motion and interaction history.

08 TURBULENT BOUNDARY LAYER

Then, the experiment gets more complex.

In a rough-wall wind tunnel, the droplets encounter sustained shear and turbulent motion instead of one isolated vortex. The flow is a controlled atmospheric-boundary-layer-like analogue, not an atmospheric replica.

09 DROPLETS IN TURBULENCE

Does the organisation survive turbulence?

Even after prolonged exposure to a turbulent boundary layer, the droplet positions remain non-random. The comparison with isolated vortices opens up questions about interaction history and clustering.

2026 APS DFD

Vortex-ring-induced clustering and collision enhancement of polydisperse droplets

Oral presentation · 24 November 2026 (scheduled) · 79th APS Division of Fluid Dynamics Annual Meeting (22–24 November 2026), Orange County Convention Center, Orlando, Florida, USA

Session: Particle-Laden Flows: Collision and Aggregation

Read the complete conference abstract +

Cloud droplet growth in warm clouds depends not only on condensation, but also on how droplet inertia and vortical structures organize droplets and promote collisions. To examine this process in a controlled setting, we study polydisperse droplets interacting with an isolated air vortex ring, used as an idealized vortical structure in turbulent clouds. High-speed imaging and particle tracking velocimetry is used to track the droplets in the vortex frame. Their spatial arrangement is quantified using Voronoi tessellation and radial and angular distribution functions, while size- and Stokes-number-resolved relative-velocity statistics describe droplet-pair motion. The measurements reveal a strongly nonuniform droplet field: droplets are depleted from the vortex-core region and accumulate in annular, asymmetric zones around the ring. These inhomogeneities coincide with enhanced radial relative velocities for selected droplet pairs, leading to increased kinematic collision kernels compared with a spatially uniform field. Event-based dynamic collision counts examine how clustering and relative motion contribute to collision efficiency. The results provide a controlled experimental link between vortical transport, preferential concentration, and droplet-collision enhancement relevant to cloud microphysics.

Authors and affiliations: Gupta Manojkumar Ishwarchandra (Indian Institute of Technology Delhi); Narsing Jha (Department of Applied Mechanics, Indian Institute of Technology Delhi, India).

Funding: This work was supported by the Prime Minister's Research Fellowship (PMRF) and the Anusandhan National Research Foundation (ANRF), Government of India.
