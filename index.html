THE QUESTION

How does a flow organise the droplets inside it—and what happens when those droplets come close?

From one controlled vortex to sustained turbulent shear.

SELECTED RESEARCH / EXPERIMENTAL WORK

Inside the experiment.

Original images, measurements and analysis from my doctoral research at IIT Delhi. A visual introduction, not the full thesis.

I

Creating and measuring a vortex

From the first component to a calibrated experimental flow.

The experiment

Built from the ground up

The vortex-ring–droplet facility was developed from scratch at IIT Delhi. Working with Prof. Narsing Kumar Jha, I contributed from material selection and fabrication through optical alignment, calibration and measurement. The aim was simple: isolate what one coherent vortex does to a cloud of droplets.

01 / 09
Flow visualisation

Making the vortex visible

Fog tracers reveal the translating toroidal structure and its evolution. The sequence also hints at how even small droplets can be redistributed around a rotating core.

02 / 09
Flow measurement

Beyond the visible structure

Time-resolved particle image velocimetry provides velocity and vorticity fields. Those measurements give the vortex its physical scales before we examine how droplets respond.

03 / 09
Vortex–droplet interaction

When a vortex meets a droplet field

A single passage produces remarkably different motions: gentle fly-bys, strong transverse deflections and near-core encounters. The outcome depends on droplet inertia, position and the path taken through the flow.

04 / 09
II

What the droplets reveal

Following motion, finding structure, and studying pair encounters.

Lagrangian dynamics

Following the droplets

Tracking individual droplets in the vortex frame of reference reveals what the laboratory view can conceal: changes in relative motion, displacement and trajectory history during the encounter.

05 / 09
Preferential concentration

Order within apparent randomness

Voronoï analysis brings out the pattern: droplet-rich regions appear alongside depleted voids. The organisation changes with distance and angle relative to the moving vortex.

06 / 09
Pair statistics

Is being close enough?

Clustering alone cannot establish whether two droplets approach each other. We combine near-contact pair concentration with radial relative motion to estimate kinematic encounter kernels—not directly observed physical collision or coalescence rates.

07 / 09
III

From one vortex to turbulence

Extending the question to a rough-wall turbulent boundary layer.

Wind-tunnel experiments

A more complex turbulent environment

The study then moves from a passing isolated vortex to a rough-wall turbulent boundary layer. Controlled mean shear and sustained turbulence offer a second perspective on droplet organisation, with selected similarities to atmospheric boundary layers.

08 / 09
Droplets in turbulence

Does the organisation persist?

Even after prolonged exposure to turbulent fluctuations and mean shear, the droplets remain non-randomly distributed. The measurements invite a broader question: how much does the history of a droplet determine where it is found?

A LITTLE ABOUT MY WORK

As interested in building the measurement as in understanding the physics.

My PhD research, Clustering and Redistribution of Polydisperse Droplets in Vortical Flows, grew alongside the development of the experimental capabilities at the FE2B Fluids Lab. 2026 APS DFD

Vortex-ring-induced clustering and collision enhancement of polydisperse droplets

Oral presentation scheduled for 24 November 2026 · 79th APS Division of Fluid Dynamics Annual Meeting, 22–24 November 2026 · Orange County Convention Center, Orlando, Florida, USA

Session: Particle-Laden Flows: Collision and Aggregation

Read conference abstract +

Cloud droplet growth in warm clouds depends not only on condensation, but also on how droplet inertia and vortical structures organize droplets and promote collisions. To examine this process in a controlled setting, we study polydisperse droplets interacting with an isolated air vortex ring, used as an idealized vortical structure in turbulent clouds. High-speed imaging and particle tracking velocimetry is used to track the droplets in the vortex frame. Their spatial arrangement is quantified using Voronoi tessellation and radial and angular distribution functions, while size- and Stokes-number-resolved relative-velocity statistics describe droplet-pair motion. The measurements reveal a strongly nonuniform droplet field: droplets are depleted from the vortex-core region and accumulate in annular, asymmetric zones around the ring. These inhomogeneities coincide with enhanced radial relative velocities for selected droplet pairs, leading to increased kinematic collision kernels compared with a spatially uniform field. Event-based dynamic collision counts examine how clustering and relative motion contribute to collision efficiency. The results provide a controlled experimental link between vortical transport, preferential concentration, and droplet-collision enhancement relevant to cloud microphysics.

Authors and affiliations: Gupta Manojkumar Ishwarchandra (Indian Institute of Technology Delhi); Narsing Jha (Department of Applied Mechanics, Indian Institute of Technology Delhi, India).
